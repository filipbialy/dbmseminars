The planes that came back

Second World War. Bombers return from missions covered in bullet holes: in the wings, the fuselage and the tail. Hardly any are in the engines.

Armour is heavy, so you can only add a little.

Where would you put it?

Armour where the holes aren't

The mathematician Abraham Wald, working with the Statistical Research Group in New York, pointed out what everyone was missing: these are the planes that came back.

A plane hit in the wings could still fly home. A plane hit in the engines usually didn't, so it never appeared in the data.

This mistake is called survivorship bias: judging from what you can see, while missing what never made it back.

What this has to do with your revision

Survivorship bias is a helpful way to think about preparing for the exam. When you prepare, you should focus on your vulnerabilities: the gaps in your knowledge. The problem is that they may be in places you aren't aware of. Such gaps are the unknown unknowns.

The check that follows is a way of making these unknowns more visible. You will be asked 30 diagnostic questions that will help you find where your vulnerabilities lie.

Note for you: story vs history

Wald's 1943 memoranda for the Statistical Research Group are real. They estimate how vulnerable each part of a plane is from damage on the planes that returned, correcting for the ones that were lost. The scene where he tells the military to armour the engines and they follow his advice isn't documented. It's a later retelling. The red-dot plane picture is from around 2005.
